The four words that do all the work

Reference
Datum
An arbitrary reference plane fixed by the manufacturer — on most Cessnas, the front face of the firewall. It is not a physical part of the aeroplane and it is not the balance point. It is simply where the tape measure reads zero.
Arm
The horizontal distance from the datum to a load, in inches. Aft of the datum is positive; forward is negative. Arms come from the POH and do not change with loading.
Moment
Weight × arm. The turning effect that a load exerts about the datum, in pound-inches. A light bag far aft can out-muscle a heavy one near the datum — that is the whole lesson.
CG
Total moment ÷ total weight. The single point at which the whole aeroplane would balance. It must fall inside the envelope for the entire flight, not just at the ramp.

Why the limits exist

Handling consequences

Centre of gravity too far forward

  • Higher stall speed — the tail carries a larger download, so the wing must lift more than the aircraft weighs.
  • Heavy in pitch, and slow to rotate. On a short field the nose may not come up at all.
  • Elevator may run out of authority in the landing flare, producing a nosewheel-first arrival.
  • Higher cruise drag and reduced range from the trimmed-out tail download.
  • Recovery from a stall is normally prompt — forward CG is the stable end of the range.

Centre of gravity too far aft

  • Longitudinal stability decays. The aircraft no longer returns to trimmed speed on its own.
  • Pitch becomes light and over-sensitive; small inputs produce large attitude changes.
  • Stall recovery may be impossible — insufficient nose-down authority to break the stall.
  • Spin recovery is markedly degraded and can become unrecoverable.
  • Slightly lower stall speed and slightly better cruise — which is exactly why aft CG is tempting and dangerous.

A trimmed aeroplane does not fly nose-up because the CG is aft. This is the most common misconception in weight and balance. In steady flight the aeroplane settles at whatever angle of attack gives lift equal to weight at that speed — so pitch attitude is set by speed and weight, not by the centre of gravity. Move the CG aft and the attitude barely changes; what changes is the elevator position needed to trim, the stick force, the stability, and the load the tailplane carries. That is why the in-flight diagram tilts with the speed slider but not with the loading sliders.

The tail load is the link between balance and performance. In most light aeroplanes the wing's lift acts behind the centre of gravity, so the tailplane must push down to hold the nose up. That download adds to what the wing has to carry: the wing lifts more than the aeroplane actually weighs. Move the CG forward and the download grows, the wing works harder, and the stall speed rises. Move it aft and the download shrinks — less drag and a slightly lower stall speed, which is exactly why an aft CG is tempting, and exactly why the aft limit exists.

Fuel burn moves the CG. In most light singles the tanks sit close to the CG, so burn-off moves it only a little — but always check the zero-fuel point plotted on the chart above. In aircraft with tanks well aft of the CG, a legal takeoff can end in an illegal landing. The dashed line on the envelope chart is the path your CG walks during the flight.
